# The Table — Website Design Proposal

> *This church exists because "almost affirming" wasn't enough.*
>
> *We believe following Jesus should never require hiding who you are.*

That pairing is the thesis of this proposal. Everything that follows is in service of that first screen — the words, the photographs, the structure around it, the pages it links to, and the pages it deliberately does not.

This document describes the recommended direction for The Table's new website. The navigation stays as currently approved; the changes live in everything that sits around and beneath it.

---

## The Moment We're Designing For

Picture a specific person at a browser — say, 10pm on a Tuesday. They haven't been to church in years. They left the last one not in anger but in a slow fade, convinced the door had closed. Tonight they typed something tentative into Google. They found The Table. Their cursor is hovering over the close button.

That moment — the moment the tab almost closes — is the moment this website has to survive. Not a welcome we'd write for everyone. A recognition we'd write for the person whose hand is on the close button.

Our primary task is to make sure that person doesn't close the tab. Everything else downstream of that is navigation.

---

## Who the Homepage Is For

The Table exists because Brett chose full affirmation over institutional compromise. That is not a side policy. It is the reason the church is here. The homepage should reflect that directly.

This means the homepage is designed for the person who most needs The Table to be real — someone who has been told by a previous church that they could attend but not lead, affirm who they were but not marry who they loved, belong but not fully. Someone for whom the phrase "almost affirming" already means something and doesn't need it explained.

People who don't carry that specific wound will still find their way in. Members, visitors, curious neighbors, people who searched for a church near them for reasons that have nothing to do with LGBTQ identity. They're welcome, and the inner pages of the site will serve them well.

But the homepage has one job: survive the almost-close moment for the person who arrived carrying something. That focus is not exclusionary — it is honest. If a visitor reads the homepage and decides the church isn't for them because it names affirmation too directly, that's not a failure of the design. That's the design doing its job. Better they leave at the homepage than hurt someone in the lobby.

---

## The Direction: Honest Warmth + Conviction

In plain English: a website that feels like a real person speaking, not a church trying to market itself. Less polished reassurance. More honest recognition.

The recommended direction has two names because it is doing two things at once.

**Honest Warmth** describes the visual and voice register. Restraint instead of polish. Specificity instead of reassurance. Real photography instead of stock. Copy that admits uncertainty. The whole site should pass one test: *does this feel unfiltered?* Not curated. Not PR-ed. Not smoothed for public consumption. If a sentence or an image feels like it's been sanded down for easier reception, it's working against the brief.

**Conviction** describes the theological posture. The site does not hedge. It does not try to offend no one. It does not treat inclusion as marketing. It is explicitly Christian — reclaiming that, not softening into vague spirituality — and explicitly affirming. First sentence states why we exist. Second sentence states what we believe. Thesis and doctrine, not slogan and tagline.

The recommended name for this direction is **Honest Warmth + Conviction**. "Unfiltered" is its one-word test.

## The Homepage Hero

> **This church exists because "almost affirming" wasn't enough.**
>
> We believe following Jesus should never require hiding who you are.

The hero does two things in sequence. The first line names the cost — the specific wound of being almost-welcomed, partially-accepted, tolerated rather than embraced. It refuses to pretend the visitor is standing on neutral ground. It meets them where they already are.

The second line names the conviction. Not a creedal statement lifted from a denominational handbook. A direct claim about what following Jesus should and shouldn't cost a person. Hammer, then open hand.

A few things this pairing does that a more neutral hero would not:

- It speaks in the voice of a church that has already had the argument about whether LGBTQ people fully belong, processed it, and made up its mind. That is a much higher trust signal than any welcome can be.
- It uses a negative construction ("wasn't enough") instead of a positive one ("we welcome everyone"). Negatives describe problems the visitor recognizes; positives describe promises they have been burned by.
- It names theology directly. The visitor whose hesitation is theological sees that this church knows what it believes. The visitor whose hesitation is identity-based sees that this church names the wound.
- It does not feature any single founder as the protagonist. The wound is the protagonist.

The design around the hero should give it breathing room. No stock photography of stained glass. No call-to-action button crowding it. One real image at most, imperfect in the way real photographs are. Let the words sit.

## Two Supporting Lenses

The recommended direction does not stand alone. Two additional design sensibilities inform specific parts of the site without competing with the primary direction.

### Open Door — for practical reconnaissance

Some first-time visitors aren't navigating trauma. They're navigating logistics. What to wear. Where to park. Whether their kids will be okay. Whether they'll be asked to stand up and introduce themselves.

This register applies to one specific page — *What Sundays Are Like* — and the permissions that live at the top of *Plan Your Visit*. On those pages, copy becomes short, practical, and close to the tone of texting a friend who asked what to expect. This is not marketing. It is reconnaissance. The visitor is not looking to be sold. They're looking for a floor plan.

### Story & Substance — for deeper pages

Once a visitor decides to stay a while, the site rewards them. Brett's founding story on its own page. Pastoral letters in their own voice. Story Sunday as a first-class content type. Sermons treated as substance, not social media. This register applies to long-form pages — not the homepage.

Both of these are lenses applied within the recommended direction, not alternatives competing with it. The homepage is Honest Warmth + Conviction. The Sundays page is Open Door. The founding story page is Story & Substance. Each surface gets the register it needs.

## Content Architecture

The site's structure supports the thesis with three load-bearing content patterns and two short, protected statements.

### Story Sunday, Surfaced

Story Sunday — members sharing how they found The Table — is the most important repeating content on the site. It is simultaneously content and proof: the clearest evidence that what the homepage promises has actually happened to specific people in this specific community.

Story Sunday gets primary navigation placement, peer-level with About or Connect. Each story stands as its own entry: a short pull quote, a name, and a photograph if the person is comfortable (no photograph if they're not). Categorized by nothing — read in the order they were shared, or find the one that catches.

If audio or video from Story Sunday exists, lean into the spoken format. Transcripts as accessibility backup. The voice is where the vulnerability lives; we don't want to flatten it.

### Brett's Founding Story, On Its Own Page

Brett's story earns a standalone page. Long enough to be real — somewhere around 600 to 900 words — and short enough to read in one sitting on a phone. Not a mission statement. A narrative with a turn:

- What he believed was possible before
- The moment it became clear it wasn't
- What he decided to build instead

First person, in his own voice where possible. The goal is not to position a founder but to put the reader inside the decision that made the church. This page is where the cost becomes visible in full — which is also why the homepage does not have to carry all of it.

### "What Sundays Are Like" as Reconnaissance

A dedicated page that answers the questions a first-time visitor actually asks themselves, in the register of logistics rather than marketing. The structure:

- **Getting there** — parking, entrances, timing
- **What it looks like inside** — dress, visual norms
- **The service itself** — what happens, what's optional, whether communion is weird
- **Kids and noise** — whether children are expected to be silent
- **People** — whether anyone will corner or follow up
- **If you need to leave** — permission stated plainly
- **What it's like if you're LGBTQ** — the actual question, answered quietly

That last section, written short and unembellished:

> Full affirmation isn't a program here. It's the baseline. Nobody will make a thing of it. You'll just be here.

This is the quietest section on the page. It's also the most important. It refuses the performative allyship register — not a special welcome, not a diversity feature, not a guest-star episode. Just presence.

### "You Can Leave Quietly" — Permission Above the Fold

At the top of *Plan Your Visit*, before service times or parking, one short note:

> You do not need to know what you believe before you come. You also do not need to stay.

The visitor this site is written for runs reconnaissance before committing. They need to know the exit exists before they will walk in. This permission is not reassurance marketing; it is logistics — stated plainly, in regular body weight. The understatement is the point.

## Visual Direction

### Photography

Real moments, not staged ones. The brief for a Sunday photographer is: *catch people mid-laugh, mid-conversation, looking messy and real.* Imperfect framing is a feature. A range of bodies, ages, and expressions — because that is what the room looks like, not because of a checklist. If someone in the congregation is visibly queer, they appear naturally in photography — not centered, not spotlit, just present.

The first question to answer about photography is: *what does The Table already have?* The existing inventory shapes every decision that follows.

### Typography

Warmth with slight idiosyncrasy. Not corporate sans-serif. Not a precious editorial serif. Something that reads as chosen by a person. Mixed type scales that feel editorial rather than templated.

### Color and Space

Restrained, not performative. Generous whitespace. Resist the visual language of other "progressive church" sites — airy sans-serif paired with muted terracotta paired with linen textures paired with a photograph of someone looking contemplative into middle distance. That aesthetic has become its own register, and the visitor this site is for has been burned by churches that looked exactly like it.

### Navigation

Retained as currently approved. Story Sunday elevated to primary navigation, peer-level with About or Connect — never buried under a Media dropdown. The rest of the structure stays.

## What This Design Refuses

Some things the site will explicitly not do. These are not design preferences — they are protections against patterns that actively harm trust for the person we are designing for.

- **No rainbow flag in the header.** Prominent badging signals wanting credit for inclusion more than providing safety. The site shows; it does not badge.
- **No stock photography of diverse smiling people.** Immediately legible as performed inclusion and triggers the same alarm as other well-intentioned churches that let visitors down.
- **No copy that over-explains acceptance.** "All are welcome here, no matter who you love" over-states the anxiety behind it.
- **No workshopped mission statement.** Anything that reads as committee-written reads as institutional.
- **No neutrality.** Neutrality in a church homepage is often fear wearing khakis. Conviction is better.

---

## What This Design Is

A homepage that behaves like an actual person speaking, not a marketing surface.

A site that expects the visitor to arrive already wary, and treats that wariness with respect rather than trying to dissolve it with warmth.

An argument — small, specific, and repeated across every page — that the visitor does not have to choose between who they are and where they belong.

A website that tells the truth about who The Table is, and trusts that the people who need it will recognize it.

---

## Next Step

With a green light on this direction, the next step is a coded prototype of the homepage plus the supporting pages named above (*Plan Your Visit*, *What Sundays Are Like*, Brett's story, Story Sunday index). Working prototype rather than static mockups — so the feel of the site can be evaluated in a real browser with real content at real speed, which is the only medium where "unfiltered" reads as intentional rather than incomplete.
